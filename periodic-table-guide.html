The periodic table: a practical guide for Elementle

The periodic table is more than a list to memorise. It is a map of atoms. Once you know what its numbers, rows and families represent, an Elementle board becomes a set of connected scientific clues instead of eight isolated guesses.

Atomic number is the table’s address system

An element is defined by its atomic number—the number of protons in its nucleus. Hydrogen has one proton, helium has two, carbon has six and gold has 79. No two elements share an atomic number, which is why it is the most reliable way to identify an element.

The table increases one atomic number at a time as you read across a row, then continues on the next row. In Elementle, the up and down arrows convert that sequence into a search boundary. A higher arrow tells you to move toward larger atomic numbers; a lower arrow tells you to move toward smaller ones. Combining two or three arrows often removes most possible answers before you need to know any detailed chemistry.

Periods are rows: electron shells in action

The horizontal rows are called periods. Elements in the same period have electrons occupying the same main set of energy levels, often called shells. As you move left to right across a period, the number of protons grows and the outer electron arrangement changes in a predictable way. That is why the row moves from reactive metals through a mixture of metals and nonmetals toward an unreactive noble gas.

Periods also explain a useful visual feature of the table: the two detached rows at the bottom are not extra elements. They are the lanthanides and actinides, inserted below the main body so the table fits comfortably on a page. They belong in periods six and seven respectively.

Groups are columns: similar chemistry repeats

Vertical columns are called groups. Their members tend to have related outer-electron arrangements, so they often react in similar ways. That repeating pattern is the source of the word periodic. In Elementle, a matching family clue can be powerful because it tells you that the answer shares one of these recurring chemical identities with your guess.

Element families worth recognising

Alkali metals

Group 1, aside from hydrogen, contains lithium, sodium, potassium and heavier relatives. These metals have one outer electron and readily react, particularly with water. They are soft compared with many other metals and are rarely found as pure elements in nature.

Alkaline earth metals

Group 2 includes magnesium and calcium. They have two outer electrons and are reactive, though generally less dramatically than the alkali metals. Magnesium is familiar from bright white flames and lightweight alloys; calcium is central to bones, shells and limestone compounds.

Transition metals

The broad central block includes iron, copper, nickel, silver and gold. Transition metals are often strong, conductive or useful in alloys. Their chemistry is varied, so a transition-metal family clue narrows the field less than a noble-gas clue, but it still provides a useful region of the table.

Metalloids and other metals

Metalloids sit along a zig-zag boundary between metals and nonmetals. Silicon and germanium are familiar examples: they can conduct electricity under the right conditions, which makes them important in electronics. The label “metal” elsewhere on the table covers several groups with different behaviour, including aluminium, tin and lead.

Nonmetals and halogens

Nonmetals include everyday elements such as carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and sulfur. They occupy much of the upper-right region and can form covalent bonds by sharing electrons. Halogens—fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine—are a distinctive group of reactive nonmetals that often form salts with metals. Chlorine in table salt is bonded with sodium, for example, even though the pure elements behave very differently.

Noble gases

Helium, neon, argon and their heavier neighbours sit in the far-right column. Their outer electron shells are especially stable, making them much less reactive than most elements. When Elementle confirms the noble-gas family, the candidate list becomes very small very quickly.

Lanthanides and actinides

The lanthanides include many elements used in magnets, lighting, optics and electronics. The actinides include thorium, uranium and several synthetic elements. Many actinides are radioactive, but radioactivity is a property of particular isotopes and should not be treated as a shortcut for every element in that row.

Symbols are clues, not always abbreviations

An element’s symbol has one capital letter or a capital letter followed by a lower-case letter. Many are easy to connect to the English name: O for oxygen, Ne for neon and Ca for calcium. Others reflect older or Latin names. Sodium is Na from natrium, potassium is K from kalium, iron is Fe from ferrum, silver is Ag from argentum and gold is Au from aurum.

This is why Elementle’s green and yellow symbol feedback matters. Once you know a correct letter or its position, you can use it with the atomic-number range and family rather than treating the symbol as a separate word puzzle.

A repeatable way to reason through a board

  1. Use the atomic-number arrows to set a lower and upper boundary.
  2. Keep any confirmed family in mind and choose the next guess from that family when it helps split the range.
  3. Record symbol letters mentally: green fixes a position, while yellow confirms a letter but asks you to move it.
  4. Use a hint to connect a scientific fact or everyday use to the remaining candidates.
  5. Make the final guesses deliberately. A smaller search range is often more valuable than a familiar-sounding name.

Keep exploring

Elementle is designed as a starting point for curiosity. The game’s facts are short clues, not laboratory or health advice. If an answer interests you, follow up with a trusted chemistry resource, a textbook or a science museum. Over time, the pattern of numbers, symbols and families becomes much easier to recognise.
